Accommodation (eye's own focusing)

A young child has a lot of accommodation and can "lens away" farsightedness, but not astigmatism: focusing one meridian throws the other one out. Try the buttons to see the two possible sharp directions.

Realism

1.0 is pure ray optics, which is known to overstate how blurry things look to the person: the brain adapts to habitual blur, and letters stay recognizable past the point geometry says they shouldn't. 0.7 lines up reasonably with published uncorrected-acuity data. The Stiles–Crawford effect (retina favors light through the pupil center) is always applied.

How the simulation works, and its limits

The refraction is split into its two principal meridians. After subtracting any accommodation, each meridian's leftover defocus (in diopters) spreads a point of light into a line whose angular length is roughly pupil diameter × defocus. Both meridians together make an elliptical blur patch, which is convolved with the image using WebGL. Sample weights follow the Stiles–Crawford effect. Even so, geometric optics overstates perceived blur (no diffraction or higher-order aberrations, and no neural adaptation), so the blur is drawn at a scale of 0.7 by default; the "20/xx" figure comes from an empirical fit to uncorrected-acuity data rather than from the picture. For an infant or toddler the retina and visual cortex are still maturing, so even a perfectly corrected eye does not see 20/20 yet.
